== Euler update
\var{Stock}(t + DT) = \var{Stock}(t) + DT \times [ \var{Inflow}(t) - \var{Outflow}(t) ]
? A stock is like a bath. The new level is the old level plus what flowed in, minus what flowed out.
? Flows are rates, so multiply by the time step DT. A smaller DT is more accurate.
> Stock 100, inflow 30, outflow 10, DT = 1.
= \var{Stock} = 100 + 1 \times (30 - 10) = 120
== Net flow
\var{Net}(t) = 30 + 20 - \var{Outflow}(t) = 50 - \var{Outflow}(t)
\var{Net} = -50 + 20t \quad \text{on [0, 5]}
\var{Net} = 150 - 20t \quad \text{on [5, 10]}
? Net flow is everything coming in minus everything going out. Here the two inflows add to 50.
? When it is positive the stock rises, when negative it falls, and the area under it is the change.
> For the first line, -50 + 20t, the net flow is negative until t = 2.5, so the stock falls by 62.5 and then climbs back.
== Net flow#2
\var{Net} = 50 - 20t \quad \text{on [0, 5]}
\var{Net} = -50 \quad \text{on [5, 10]}
\var{Net} = -50 + 20(t - 10) \quad \text{on [10, 15]}
\var{Net} = +50 \quad \text{on [15, 20]}
\var{Net} = 50 - 20(t - 20) \quad \text{on [20, 25]}
? Each line covers one stretch of the graph. The stock changes by the area under that stretch.
? Where the net flow crosses zero, the stock is at a peak or a low point.
> On the first line, 50 - 20t, the stock rises until t = 2.5 (area 62.5), then falls back by the same amount.
== Stock management structure
\text{Inflow (Hire Rate)} = \var{MAX}(0, \var{Anchor} + \var{Adjustment})
? To manage a stock, first replace what you expect to lose (anchor), then correct the gap to your target (adjustment).
? MAX with 0 stops the answer going negative: you cannot hire minus people.
> Anchor 10 and adjustment 5 give 15 hires. If the adjustment were -20, hires would be 0.
== Adjustment
\var{Adjustment} = \frac{\text{Desired Stock} - \var{Stock}}{AT}
~ AT = adjustment time.
? The gap between where you want to be and where you are, closed gradually over the adjustment time.
> Target 100, current 80, adjustment time 4.
= \var{Adjustment} = \frac{100 - 80}{4} = 5
== Anchor
\var{Anchor} = \text{Expected Outflow}
~ The smoothed outflow. In steady state Anchor = Outflow.
? Even with no gap, things keep leaving the stock. The anchor replaces that expected loss.
? Without it the stock would settle below the target.
> If you expect to lose 10 staff a month, start by hiring 10.
== Anchor delay structure
\var{EQ}(t + DT) = \var{EQ}(t) + DT \times \frac{\var{Quits} - \var{EQ}}{TAE}
~ EQ = Expected Quits. TAE = Time to Adjust Expectation.
? Your expectation does not jump to the latest figure. It moves part of the way each step.
? A longer adjustment time means slower, smoother beliefs.
> Expected quits 10, actual 14, time to adjust 4, DT = 1.
= \var{EQ} = 10 + 1 \times \frac{14 - 10}{4} = 11
== Transmission rate beta (per day)
\beta = c \times i = \frac{R_0}{D}
? Beta is how many people one infected person infects per day when everyone is susceptible.
? Contacts per day times the chance each passes it on, which equals R0 spread over the infectious days.
> R0 = 12 over 7 infectious days.
= \beta = \frac{12}{7} = 1.71
== Force of infection lambda (per day, per susceptible)
\lambda = \beta \times \frac{I}{N}
~ The probability per day that a susceptible person is infected.
? A susceptible person's daily risk: the transmission rate times the share of people who are infected.
> Beta 0.5, 100 infected out of 10,000.
= \lambda = 0.5 \times \frac{100}{10000} = 0.005
== Infection rate
\var{IR} = S \times \lambda = \frac{\beta \times S \times I}{N} = c \times i \times \frac{S}{N} \times I
~ IR = Infection Rate.
? New infections per day: the number who can catch it times each one's daily risk.
? It is small at the start (few infected) and at the end (few left to infect), and largest in between.
> 9000 susceptible with risk 0.005.
= \var{IR} = 9000 \times 0.005 = 45
== Net (effective) reproduction number
R_n = R_0 \times \frac{S}{N}
~ Changes over time as S falls.
? R0 assumes everyone can catch it. Only the susceptible share still can.
? Above 1 the outbreak grows, below 1 it shrinks.
> R0 = 3 with half the people still susceptible.
= R_n = 3 \times 0.5 = 1.5
== Herd immunity threshold
\var{HIT} = 1 - \frac{1}{R_0}
? The share who must be immune so that each case infects fewer than one other.
? A more contagious disease needs a higher share.
> R0 = 12.
= \var{HIT} = 1 - \frac{1}{12} = 0.92
== Differential form
\frac{dS}{dt} = - \frac{\beta S I}{N}
\frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{\beta S I}{N} - \frac{I}{D}
\frac{dR}{dt} = \frac{I}{D}
? People move one way: Susceptible to Infected to Recovered.
? What leaves S enters I, and what leaves I (after D days on average) enters R.
> Beta 0.5, S = 9900, I = 100, N = 10,000, D = 5. S changes by -49.5 a day, I by +29.5 and R by +20.
== Difference (Euler) form
S(t + DT) = S(t) - DT \times \var{IR}(t)
I(t + DT) = I(t) + DT \times (\var{IR}(t) - \var{RR}(t))
R(t + DT) = R(t) + DT \times \var{RR}(t)
? The same model computed in time steps: each box is its old value plus or minus the flows times DT.
> S = 9900, I = 100, infections 49.5, recoveries 20, DT = 1. Next: S = 9850.5, I = 129.5, R = 20.
== Fractional increase
\var{Inflow} = \var{Stock} \times g
~ g = fractional growth rate (per time unit).
? The inflow is a fixed fraction of the stock, so the more you have the faster it grows. This gives exponential growth.
> A stock of 1000 growing at 0.05 per year gains 50 this year.
== Fractional decrease
\var{Outflow} = \var{Stock} \times f = \frac{\var{Stock}}{L}
~ f = fractional decrease rate. L = 1/f = average lifetime.
? The outflow is a fixed fraction of what is there, so it slows as the stock empties.
? A fraction of 1/L means things last L time units on average.
> 1000 items with an average life of 20 years: 50 leave per year.
== Adjustment to a goal
\text{Net flow} = \frac{\var{Goal} - \var{Stock}}{AT}
~ AT = adjustment time (time constant).
? The further from the goal, the faster you move. You slow down as you get close.
> Goal 100, stock 60, adjustment time 5.
= \var{Net} = \frac{100 - 60}{5} = 8
== Equations
\var{Outflow} = S \times G
G(t + DT) = G(t) + DT \times \frac{G^{*} - G}{AT}
~ G(0) = the initial fraction (for example 0).
? The outflow is a fraction G of the stock, but that fraction is not fixed.
? G starts at its initial value and drifts towards the target fraction, taking about AT to get there.
> G = 0, target 0.1, AT = 5, DT = 1: next G is 0.02. With a stock of 1000 the outflow is then 20.
== First-order material delay
\var{Outflow} = \frac{\var{Stock}}{D}
\text{steady state: } \var{Stock} = \var{Inflow} \times D
? Things enter and leave after about D time units on average, like parcels in the post.
? When nothing is changing, the amount in the middle is the inflow times the delay.
> 20 enter per day and stay 5 days, so 100 are in the system and 20 leave per day.
== Exponential smoothing
\var{Sm}(t + DT) = \var{Sm}(t) + DT \times \frac{\var{Input} - \var{Sm}(t)}{ST}
~ Sm = the smoothed value. ST = smoothing (perception) time.
? The smoothed value chases the real one, closing part of the gap each step, so sudden spikes are softened.
> Smoothed 100, input 120, smoothing time 4, DT = 1.
= \var{Sm} = 100 + \frac{120 - 100}{4} = 105
== Cohort force of infection
\lambda_j = i \times \sum_{k} c_{jk} \times \frac{I_k}{N_k}
\var{IR}_j = S_j \times \lambda_j \quad \var{RR}_j = \frac{I_j}{D_j}
? With age groups, each group's risk adds up its contacts with every group, times the share infected there.
? The group's infections are its susceptibles times that risk.
> Chance per contact 0.05. You meet 10 of one group (1% infected) and 2 of another (2% infected). 1000 susceptible.
= \lambda = 0.05 (10 \times 0.01 + 2 \times 0.02) = 0.007
> That gives 1000 × 0.007 = 7 infections a day.
== Stock equation (Euler form)
\var{Stock}(t + DT) = \var{Stock}(t) + DT \times (\var{Inflow} - \var{Outflow})
? The new level is the old level plus the net flow over one time step.
> Stock 50, inflow 10, outflow 4, DT = 0.5.
= \var{Stock} = 50 + 0.5 \times (10 - 4) = 53
== Continuous form
\frac{d(\var{Stock})}{dt} = \var{Inflow} - \var{Outflow}
\var{Stock}(0) = \text{initial value}
? The same idea for infinitely small steps: how fast the stock changes right now is inflow minus outflow.
? You also need the starting level, or you only know the shape, not the height.
> Inflow 10 and outflow 4: the stock rises by 6 per time unit from wherever it started.
== SEIR
\frac{dS}{dt} = - \frac{\beta S I}{N}
\frac{dE}{dt} = \frac{\beta S I}{N} - \frac{E}{L}
\frac{dI}{dt} = \frac{E}{L} - \frac{I}{D}
\frac{dR}{dt} = \frac{I}{D}
~ L = latent time.
? SIR with a waiting room: E holds people who caught it but are not yet infectious.
? They move to I after about L days, which delays and spreads out the outbreak.
> Beta 0.5, S = 990, E = 20, I = 10, N = 1000, L = 2, D = 5. E changes by 4.95 - 10 = -5.05 and I by 10 - 2 = +8.
== Force of infection (three infectious compartments)
\lambda = c \times i \times \frac{\text{Pre-clinical} + \var{Clinical} + M \times \text{Sub-clinical}}{N}
~ M = the sub-clinical multiplier.
? Three kinds of people can pass it on. Add them, counting sub-clinical cases at M times the strength.
? Then multiply by contacts and chance per contact, divided by the population.
> c × i = 0.6, 10 pre-clinical, 20 clinical, 30 sub-clinical, M = 0.5, N = 1000.
= \lambda = 0.6 \times \frac{10 + 20 + 15}{1000} = 0.027
== R0 for this model
R_0 = c i [ CF (2.1 + 2.9) + (1 - CF) \times 0.5 \times 5 ]
\quad = 0.6 \times [ 0.6 \times 5 + 0.4 \times 2.5 ] = 0.6 \times 4 = 2.4
? One case's total infections: infections per day times days infectious, averaged over the two routes.
? A fraction CF turns clinical (2.1 + 2.9 days). The rest stay sub-clinical (5 days at half strength).
> With CF = 0.6: 0.6 × (0.6 × 5 + 0.4 × 2.5) = 2.4.
