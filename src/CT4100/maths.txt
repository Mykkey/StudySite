== Pearson correlation between active user a and user u
w(a,u) = \frac{\sum_{i} (r(a,i) - \bar{r}_a)(r(u,i) - \bar{r}_u)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i} (r(a,i) - \bar{r}_a)^2 \times \sum_{i} (r(u,i) - \bar{r}_u)^2}}
~ The sums are over i in I(a,u), the items rated by both. w is between -1 and 1.
? It answers: do these two people's ratings go up and down together?
? First take away each person's own average, so a number says "more than usual" (+) or "less than usual" (-).
? Multiply the two numbers for each item both rated. Agreeing gives a positive result, disagreeing a negative one. Add them up.
? The bottom only rescales the total to lie between -1 (opposite tastes) and 1 (same tastes).
> Ann rates three films 5, 3, 1. Bob rates them 4, 3, 2. Both average 3.
> Take away the averages: Ann is +2, 0, -2 and Bob is +1, 0, -1.
= w = \frac{2 + 0 + 2}{\sqrt{8 \times 2}} = 1
> Bob is less extreme than Ann, but their ups and downs match exactly, so the score is 1.
== Cosine (vector) similarity
\cos(a,u) = \frac{a \cdot u}{|a| |u|}
~ Users are rating vectors, with 0 for "not rated".
? Treat each person's ratings as an arrow, and measure how closely the two arrows point the same way.
? The top is large when both people gave high ratings to the same items.
? The bottom removes the effect of how long each arrow is, so someone who rates a lot is not automatically "more similar".
> Three films, 0 means not rated. Ann is (5, 3, 0) and Bob is (4, 0, 2).
= \cos = \frac{5 \times 4 + 3 \times 0 + 0 \times 2}{\sqrt{34} \times \sqrt{20}} = 0.77
> 1 would mean the same direction. 0 would mean no rated film in common.
== Prediction 1: weighted average of ratings
P(a,i) = \frac{\sum_{u} r(u,i) w(a,u)}{\sum_{u} |w(a,u)|}
~ Assumes everyone uses the rating scale in the same way.
? You want to guess what person a would give item i. Ask other people u who have rated it.
? Do not count everyone equally. w(a,u) says how similar u is to a, so a similar person gets a bigger say.
? The top multiplies each rating by that say and adds them up.
? The bottom adds up all the say. Dividing by it brings the answer back to the rating scale (without it, more neighbours would just mean a bigger number).
? Why it is a good guess: people who agreed with you before are likely to agree with you again.
? Weakness: a negative similarity subtracts a rating and can give silly results, so use neighbours with positive similarity here.
> You are guessing Ann's rating for a film. Bob is very like Ann (0.9) and gave it 5. Cat is hardly like Ann (0.1) and gave it 2.
= P = \frac{5 \times 0.9 + 2 \times 0.1}{0.9 + 0.1} = 4.7
> A plain average would say 3.5. The weighted one says 4.7, close to Bob, because Bob's taste is the one that matches Ann's.
== Prediction 2: mean-centred weighted average (preferred)
P(a,i) = \bar{r}_a + \frac{\sum_{u} (r(u,i) - \bar{r}_u) w(a,u)}{\sum_{u} |w(a,u)|}
~ The sums are over the n neighbours who rated i.
? Some people give high marks to everything and others are harsh. A 4 does not mean the same from both.
? So for each neighbour, use how far above or below **their own** average they rated the item.
? Take the weighted average of those differences, with similar people counting more.
? Add the result to **your** average, which puts the guess on your own scale.
> Ann averages 3. Bob (similarity 0.9) averages 4 and gave the film 5, so he is +1. Cat (similarity 0.1) averages 2 and gave it 1, so Cat is -1.
= P = 3 + \frac{(+1) \times 0.9 + (-1) \times 0.1}{0.9 + 0.1} = 3.8
> Bob liked it more than usual and Bob counts most, so the guess is above Ann's usual 3.
== Significance weighting (Herlocker 1999)
w'(a,u) = \frac{\min(|I(a,u)|, 50)}{50} \times w(a,u)
? Two people can look very alike by luck when they have only a few items in common.
? So shrink the similarity when there are fewer than 50 shared items.
? With 50 or more shared items the fraction is 1 and nothing changes.
> Two people have similarity 0.9 but only 5 films in common.
= w' = \frac{5}{50} \times 0.9 = 0.09
> With 60 films in common it would stay at 0.9.
== Adjusted cosine item similarity
s(i,j) = \frac{\sum_{u} (r(u,i) - \bar{r}_u)(r(u,j) - \bar{r}_u)}{\sqrt{\sum_{u} (r(u,i) - \bar{r}_u)^2 \times \sum_{u} (r(u,j) - \bar{r}_u)^2}}
~ The sums are over users who rated both i and j.
? It asks whether two items tend to be liked by the same people.
? Each rating has that person's own average taken away first, so generous and harsh raters are treated fairly.
? If people who rated item i above their usual also rate item j above their usual, the score is near 1.
> Two people rate films i and j. Compared with their own averages, the first is +2 and +1, the second -2 and -1.
= s = \frac{(2)(1) + (-2)(-1)}{\sqrt{8 \times 2}} = 1
== Item-item prediction
P(a,i) = \frac{\sum_{j} s(i,j) r(a,j)}{\sum_{j} |s(i,j)|}
~ j runs over the k items most similar to i that a has rated. With raw ratings, use only positive similarities.
? To guess your rating for an item, look at your own ratings of items like it.
? The more alike an item is, the more your rating of it counts. Dividing by the total similarity keeps the answer on the rating scale.
> You rated a similar film (0.8) 5 and a less similar one (0.2) 3.
= P = \frac{0.8 \times 5 + 0.2 \times 3}{0.8 + 0.2} = 4.6
== AvgIDF / MaxIDF
\var{AvgIDF}(q) = \frac{1}{|q|} \sum_{t \in q} \log \frac{N}{df(t)}
\var{MaxIDF}(q) = \max_{t} \log \frac{N}{df(t)}
~ N = documents in the collection, df(t) = documents containing t. High = specific = easier.
? A rare word narrows the search down, so a query with rare words is usually easier to answer well.
? Each word scores the log of N / df: rare words score high. AvgIDF averages the scores, MaxIDF keeps the best one.
> 1000 documents. One query word is in 10 of them (score 2), the other in 100 (score 1), using log base 10.
> AvgIDF is 1.5 and MaxIDF is 2.
== Query scope
\var{Scope}(q) = \frac{|\{ d \in C : d \text{ contains a query term} \}|}{|C|}
~ "computer science": 8000/10000 = 0.80 (broad). "algorthim recusion": 30/10000 = 0.003 (misspelling or missing coverage).
? It is the share of the collection that matches at least one query word.
? A large share means the query is vague. A tiny share can mean a typo or a missing topic.
> A query matches 30 of 10,000 documents.
= \var{Scope} = \frac{30}{10000} = 0.003
== Term relatedness: average pointwise mutual information
\var{PMI}(t_1,t_2) = \log \frac{P(t_1,t_2)}{P(t_1) P(t_2)}
P(t) = \frac{df(t)}{N} \quad P(t_1,t_2) = \frac{df(t_1,t_2)}{N}
~ The predictor is the average PMI over all pairs of query terms. "neural network training" (terms co-occur) beats "neural banana training". Undefined for single-term queries.
? It asks whether two words appear together more often than chance would give.
? The bottom is the chance of seeing both if they had nothing to do with each other.
? Above 0 means they belong together, 0 means chance, below 0 means they avoid each other.
> One word is in 1% of documents, the other in 2%, and both in 0.2%. Log base 10.
= \var{PMI} = \log \frac{0.002}{0.01 \times 0.02} = \log 10 = 1
== Collection-query similarity (SCQ, Zhao et al.; extra, not on the slides)
\var{SCQ}(t) = (1 + \ln cf(t)) \times \ln (1 + \frac{N}{df(t)})
~ SCQ(q) = the sum or max over query terms. cf = collection frequency. Higher = the query is more similar to the collection = easier.
? It rewards a query word that is used a lot overall (first part) but sits in few documents (second part).
? Such a word is concentrated in particular documents, which makes them easy to pick out.
> A word occurs 100 times in total, in 50 of 1000 documents.
= \var{SCQ} = (1 + \ln 100) \times \ln (1 + 20) = 17.07
== Clarity score
\var{Clarity}(q) = \sum_{w} P(w|Q) \log_2 \frac{P(w|Q)}{P(w|C)}
~ P(w|Q) is the word distribution of the top-k retrieved documents (weighted by retrieval score). P(w|C) is the collection distribution.
? It compares the words in the top results with the words in the whole collection.
? If the results use words very differently from average, they are clearly about something, so the query was clear.
? A score near 0 means the results look like a random sample.
> A two-word vocabulary. The results use the words 80% and 20%, the collection 50% and 50%.
= \var{Clarity} = 0.8 \log_2 1.6 + 0.2 \log_2 0.4 = 0.28
== Normalised Query Commitment (NQC)
\var{NQC}(q) = \frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{k} \sum_{d \in \text{top-k}} (s(d) - \mu)^2}}{|s(C)|}
~ s(d) = retrieval score of d, μ = mean of the top-k scores, s(C) = score of the collection treated as one document (the normaliser).
? The top part is the spread (standard deviation) of the top retrieval scores.
? A wide spread means some documents clearly stand out, which suggests a good result. Flat scores suggest guessing.
? Dividing by the collection's score makes different queries comparable.
> Top scores 5, 3, 1 (mean 3), collection score 2.
= \var{NQC} = \frac{\sqrt{(4 + 0 + 4) / 3}}{2} = 0.82
== Statistical accuracy
\var{MAE} = \frac{1}{|T|} \sum |p(u,i) - r(u,i)|
\var{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|T|} \sum (p(u,i) - r(u,i))^2}
~ Over the test set T. RMSE punishes large errors more (Netflix prize metric).
? Both measure how far predicted ratings are from real ones. Lower is better.
? MAE is the plain average miss. RMSE squares the misses first, so one big miss hurts much more.
> The misses are 1, 1 and 4.
= \var{MAE} = \frac{1 + 1 + 4}{3} = 2
= \var{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 1 + 16}{3}} = 2.45
== Ideal query vector (needs complete relevance knowledge)
q_{ideal} = \frac{1}{|C_R|} \sum_{d \in C_R} d - \frac{1}{N - |C_R|} \sum_{d \notin C_R} d
~ The difference of the two centroids.
? Take the average relevant document and subtract the average non-relevant one.
? The result points towards what relevant documents have and others do not.
? It is ideal only in theory, because you would need to know which documents are relevant already.
> With two words: the relevant average is (0.75, 0.25) and the non-relevant average is (0, 1).
> The ideal query is (0.75, -0.75): want the first word, avoid the second.
== Rocchio
q_{new} = \alpha q_{orig} + \frac{\beta}{|D_R|} \sum_{d \in D_R} d - \frac{\gamma}{|D_N|} \sum_{d \in D_N} d
? Keep some of the old query, move towards documents marked relevant, and move away from the non-relevant ones.
? The three Greek letters say how much each of those three matters.
> Weights 1, 0.75 and 0.25. Old query (1, 0), relevant average (0, 1), non-relevant average (1, 0).
= q_{new} = (1, 0) + 0.75 (0, 1) - 0.25 (1, 0) = (0.75, 0.75)
> The second word, which only relevant documents had, is now in the query.
== Graded Rocchio
q_{new} = \alpha q_{orig} + \beta \frac{\sum_{d : f(d) > 0} f(d) d}{\sum f(d)} - \gamma \frac{\sum_{d : f(d) < 0} |f(d)| d}{\sum |f(d)|}
? The same idea as Rocchio, but feedback has strength: a document rated very relevant pulls harder than one rated slightly relevant.
? Each document is multiplied by its grade, and dividing by the total grades gives a weighted average.
> One document (1, 0) has grade 2 and another (0, 1) has grade 1.
> The weighted average is (0.67, 0.33), leaning towards the better-rated one.
== Association cluster (Jaccard over documents)
M(i,j) = \frac{f(i,j)}{f(i) + f(j) - f(i,j)}
~ f(i) = number of local documents containing term i. f(i,j) = number containing both.
? It scores how often two words share a document, out of all documents that have either one.
? 0 means never together, 1 means always together.
> One word is in 4 documents, another in 6, and 2 documents have both.
= M = \frac{2}{4 + 6 - 2} = 0.25
== Metric cluster (proximity)
M(i,j) = \sum_{(t_i, t_j)} \frac{1}{dist(t_i, t_j)}
~ Summed over pairs of occurrences. Closer occurrences contribute more.
? Words that appear close together are more likely to be related than words far apart in the same document.
? Each pair of occurrences adds 1 divided by the gap, so a small gap adds a lot.
> Two pairs of occurrences, 1 word apart and 4 words apart.
= M = \frac{1}{1} + \frac{1}{4} = 1.25
== Scalar cluster (similar contexts)
v_i = (M(i,1), \dots, M(i,n))
S(t_i,t_j) = \frac{v_i \cdot v_j}{|v_i| |v_j|}
~ Terms that co-occur with the same other terms are related even if they never appear together (synonyms).
? Describe each word by the list of how strongly it goes with every other word.
? Then compare the two lists with cosine similarity. Words with the same company are related, even if never seen together.
> One word's list is (2, 1) and another's is (4, 2). They point the same way, so S = 1.
== RankNet pairwise loss
P(i \triangleright j) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-(s_i - s_j))}
\var{loss} = - \log P(i \triangleright j)
~ The loss applies when i is truly more relevant. s = model score.
? The model gives two documents scores. The gap is turned into the probability that it puts them in the right order.
? The loss is small when that probability is near 1, and large when the model prefers the wrong document.
> The truly better document scores 2 points higher.
= P = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-2)} = 0.88
= \var{loss} = - \ln 0.88 = 0.13
== Pointwise loss (regression)
L = \sum_{(q,d)} (f(x_{q,d}) - y_{q,d})^2
~ Example: label 3, prediction 2.4 gives loss 0.36.
? Treat ranking as guessing each document's relevance label one at a time.
? Add up the squared misses. Training changes the model to make this total small.
> The true label is 3 and the model says 2.4.
= L = (2.4 - 3)^2 = 0.36
== MMR
\var{next} = \argmax_{d \in C \setminus S} [ \lambda \cdot rel(d,q) - (1 - \lambda) \cdot \max_{d_j \in S} sim(d, d_j) ]
~ C = candidates. S = items already selected. λ is between 0 and 1 (1 = pure relevance, 0 = pure novelty).
? It picks results one at a time, rewarding relevance and punishing being a near copy of something already picked.
? This gives a list that is useful and varied.
> Weight 0.7. Item A has relevance 0.9 but is 0.8 similar to a chosen item. Item B has relevance 0.7 and is 0.1 similar.
= A = 0.7 \times 0.9 - 0.3 \times 0.8 = 0.39
= B = 0.7 \times 0.7 - 0.3 \times 0.1 = 0.46
> B is picked, although A is more relevant on its own.
== Precision and recall
\var{Precision} = \frac{|R \cap A|}{|A|} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
\var{Recall} = \frac{|R \cap A|}{|R|} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
? Precision: of what the system returned, how much is relevant.
? Recall: of everything relevant, how much the system returned.
? Returning more usually raises recall and lowers precision, so quote both.
> 10 results, 6 of them relevant, and 12 relevant documents exist. Precision is 6/10 = 0.6 and recall is 6/12 = 0.5.
== F-measure (weighted harmonic mean)
F_{\beta} = \frac{(\beta^2 + 1) P R}{\beta^2 P + R}
F_1 = \frac{2 P R}{P + R}
~ β > 1 emphasises recall, β < 1 emphasises precision.
? It rolls precision and recall into one number.
? This kind of average stays low if either one is low, so you cannot cheat by maximising just one.
> Precision 0.6, recall 0.5.
= F_1 = \frac{2 \times 0.6 \times 0.5}{0.6 + 0.5} = 0.55
== Basic tf-idf (Spärck Jones / Salton & Fox)
w(t,d) = f(t,d) \times \log \frac{N}{n_t}
~ f = (possibly normalised) frequency of t in d, N = number of documents, n_t = number of documents containing t.
? A word matters to a document if it appears often there (first part) but in few documents overall (log part).
? A word found everywhere gets a weight near 0, because it cannot tell documents apart.
> A word appears 3 times in a document and is in 10 of 1000 documents. Log base 10.
= w = 3 \times \log \frac{1000}{10} = 6
== Max-tf normalised document weight (Salton & Buckley)
w(t,d) = \frac{tf(t,d)}{\max_{s} tf(s,d)} \times \log \frac{N}{df(t)}
? Long documents repeat words more, so each count is divided by the count of the document's most frequent word.
? Then it is multiplied by the rarity score as usual.
> The word appears 2 times, the top word 4 times, and the rarity score is 2.
= w = \frac{2}{4} \times 2 = 1
== Query weight (Salton & Buckley)
w(t,q) = (0.5 + 0.5 \times \frac{tf(t,q)}{\max_{s} tf(s,q)}) \times \log \frac{N}{df(t)}
~ Every query term starts at 0.5 and rises with its frequency in the query.
? Queries are short, so counts say little. Every query word starts at half strength and gains up to the other half by being repeated.
? That is then multiplied by the rarity score.
> A word appears once, the most frequent query word twice, and the rarity score is 2.
= w = (0.5 + 0.5 \times \frac{1}{2}) \times 2 = 1.5
== Sublinear tf
w(t,d) = 1 + \log tf(t,d) \quad \text{if tf > 0, else 0}
~ Base 10: tf 1, 10, 30, 100 gives 1, 2, 2.48, 3. 30 occurrences is not 30 times as significant as 1.
? Seeing a word 100 times does not make a document 100 times more about it.
? The log makes each extra repeat count for less.
> A word appears 100 times. Log base 10.
= w = 1 + \log 100 = 3
== Local x global x query
sim(q,d) = \sum_{t \in q \cap d} ntf(t,d) \times gwt(t) \times qwt(t,q)
~ ntf = normalised local frequency, gwt = global weight (idf), qwt = weight of t in the query.
? For each word shared by query and document, multiply three things and add up the results.
? The three are: how much the document uses it, how rare it is overall, and how much the query stresses it.
> Two shared words: 0.5 × 2 × 1 for the first and 1 × 1 × 1 for the second, so the score is 2.
== Cosine similarity
sim(q,d) = \frac{q \cdot d}{|q| |d|} = \frac{\sum_{t} w(t,q) w(t,d)}{\sqrt{\sum_{t} w(t,q)^2} \times \sqrt{\sum_{t} w(t,d)^2}}
~ Range 0 to 1 for non-negative weights.
? The query and document are arrows with one direction per word. The score is how closely they point the same way.
? Dividing by the lengths stops long documents winning just for being long.
> Query weights (1, 1), document weights (3, 4).
= sim = \frac{1 \times 3 + 1 \times 4}{\sqrt{2} \times \sqrt{25}} = 0.99
== BM25 score of document d for query q
score(q,d) = \sum_{t \in q} \log \frac{N - df_t + 0.5}{df_t + 0.5}
\quad \times \frac{tf_{t,d} (k_1 + 1)}{tf_{t,d} + k_1 ((1 - b) + b \frac{|d|}{avgdl})} \times tf_{t,q}
~ One formula over two lines: the second line is inside the sum.
? For each query word: how rare it is, times how often the document uses it, then add up.
? Repeats help less and less, and long documents are marked down. k1 controls the first effect and b the second.
> 1000 documents, the word is in 10, appears twice in an average-length document, k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75. Natural log.
= score = \ln \frac{990.5}{10.5} \times \frac{2 \times 2.2}{2 + 1.2} = 6.25
== Precision at k
P@k = \frac{\text{relevant documents in top k}}{k}
\var{R-precision} = P@R
? People look at the first few results, so only the top k are judged.
? R-precision sets k to the number of relevant documents that exist, so a perfect system scores 1.
> 3 of the top 5 results are relevant.
= P@5 = \frac{3}{5} = 0.6
== Average precision (AP) and MAP
\var{AP}(q) = \frac{1}{|R_q|} \sum_{k : d_k \text{ relevant}} P@k
\var{MAP} = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q} \var{AP}(q)
~ Divide by ALL relevant documents, so unretrieved relevant documents count as precision 0.
? Each time a relevant document appears, note the precision at that point, then average over all relevant documents.
? Finding them early scores higher. MAP is the average of this over many queries.
> 3 relevant documents exist. Two are found, at ranks 1 and 3, and one is missed.
= \var{AP} = \frac{1 + \frac{2}{3} + 0}{3} = 0.56
== Mean reciprocal rank
\var{MRR} = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q} \frac{1}{rank_q}
~ rank_q = rank of the first relevant document for query q.
? It cares only about how soon the first good answer appears.
? Rank 1 scores 1, rank 2 scores a half, rank 4 a quarter. Then average over queries.
> First relevant results at ranks 1, 2 and 4.
= \var{MRR} = \frac{1 + 0.5 + 0.25}{3} = 0.58
== DCG and nDCG
\var{DCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{rel_i}{\log_2 (i + 1)}
\var{nDCG}@k = \frac{\var{DCG}@k}{\var{IDCG}@k}
~ IDCG = DCG of the ideal ordering (sorted by relevance). nDCG is between 0 and 1. A variant uses gain 2^rel - 1.
? Each result adds its relevance, shrunk the lower it appears, so good results near the top count most.
? Dividing by the best possible order gives a score from 0 to 1 that can be compared across queries.
> Relevance in ranked order: 3, 0, 2. Best order: 3, 2, 0.
= \var{DCG} = 3 + 0 + \frac{2}{\log_2 4} = 4
= \var{IDCG} = 3 + \frac{2}{\log_2 3} = 4.26
= \var{nDCG} = \frac{4}{4.26} = 0.94
== Purity
\var{purity}(\Omega, C) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k} \max_{j} |\omega_k \cap c_j|
~ For each cluster take the count of its majority class, add them up, divide by the total number of points.
? In each cluster, count the items belonging to its most common true class. Add those counts and divide by all items.
? 1 means every cluster is one class. Beware: very many tiny clusters also score high.
> 10 items in 2 clusters. The majority counts are 4 and 3.
= \var{purity} = \frac{4 + 3}{10} = 0.7
== Rand index (pair counting)
\var{RI} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FP + FN + TN}
~ Over all N(N-1)/2 document pairs. TP = same class and same cluster; FP = different classes but same cluster; FN = same class but different clusters; TN = different in both.
? Look at every pair of items and ask whether the clustering made the right call: together or apart.
? The score is the share of pairs it got right.
> Of 6 pairs: 2 correctly together, 2 correctly apart, and 2 wrong.
= \var{RI} = \frac{2 + 2}{6} = 0.67
== p-norm similarity for two terms (p between 1 and infinity)
sim(d, t_1 \text{ OR } t_2) = (\frac{w_1^p + w_2^p}{2})^{\frac{1}{p}}
sim(d, t_1 \text{ AND } t_2) = 1 - (\frac{(1 - w_1)^p + (1 - w_2)^p}{2})^{\frac{1}{p}}
~ NOT t has weight (1 - w).
? It is a softer version of Boolean OR and AND, so partial matches still get a score.
? OR is high if either word matches. AND is high only when both are close to 1.
? At p = 1 the two behave like a plain average. As p grows they behave more like strict Boolean logic.
> p = 2, the document has the first word (weight 1) but not the second (weight 0).
= \var{OR} = \sqrt{\frac{1 + 0}{2}} = 0.71
= \var{AND} = 1 - \sqrt{\frac{0 + 1}{2}} = 0.29
== PageRank
\var{PR}(W) = \frac{T}{N} + (1 - T) [ \frac{\var{PR}(W_1)}{O(W_1)} + \frac{\var{PR}(W_2)}{O(W_2)} + \dots + \frac{\var{PR}(W_n)}{O(W_n)} ]
~ W_i = pages linking to W; O(W_i) = number of outlinks of W_i; T = teleportation probability (damping factor d = 1 - T, typically 0.85); N = number of pages.
? Imagine someone clicking links at random, who sometimes jumps to any page. A page's score is how often they land on it.
? Each page linking to W passes it a share of its own score, split between all its outlinks.
? So links from important, choosy pages are worth most.
> Jump chance 0.15, 4 pages. W is linked by a page scoring 0.25 with 2 links, and one scoring 0.25 with 1 link.
= \var{PR} = \frac{0.15}{4} + 0.85 (\frac{0.25}{2} + \frac{0.25}{1}) = 0.36
== Matrix form
a \leftarrow M^T h \quad h \leftarrow M a
a \propto M^T M a \quad h \propto M M^T h
~ M is the adjacency matrix (M_ij = 1 if page i links to page j). Repeated multiplication (power method) converges to the principal eigenvector of M^T M (authorities) and of M M^T (hubs).
? This is HITS. A good authority is linked to by good hubs, and a good hub links to good authorities.
? So each score is worked out from the other, over and over, until they stop changing (rescaling each round).
> Pages A and B both link to C. Starting hubs at 1, C gets authority 2. Then A and B each get hub score 2.
== Coverage and novelty
\var{Coverage} = \frac{|AU|}{|U|}
\var{Novelty} = \frac{|New|}{|New| + |AU|}
? Coverage: of the relevant documents you already knew about, how many did the search find.
? Novelty: of the relevant documents it found, how many were new to you.
> You knew of 100 and it found 80 of those, so coverage is 0.8. Of 10 relevant results, 3 are new, so novelty is 0.3.
== Zipf
f \times r \approx \text{constant} \quad f \approx \frac{C}{r}
f \propto r^{-\alpha} \quad \alpha \approx 1
~ A straight line on a log-log plot. r = rank by frequency, f = frequency.
? In any large text, a few words are very common and most are rare.
? The second most common word appears about half as often as the first, the tenth about a tenth as often.
> If the top word appears 1000 times, expect about 500 for rank 2 and 100 for rank 10.
== Heaps
M = k T^{\beta}
~ M = vocabulary size (types), T = collection size in tokens, typically 10 ≤ k ≤ 100 and 0.4 ≤ β ≤ 0.6.
? As you read more text, you keep meeting new words, but more and more slowly.
? So an index's word list grows much more slowly than the collection.
> k = 10, exponent 0.5, one million words of text.
= M = 10 \times \sqrt{1000000} = 10000
== Worked example: Pearson
w = \frac{\sum (a - \bar{a})(u - \bar{u})}{\sqrt{\sum (a - \bar{a})^2 \times \sum (u - \bar{u})^2}}
~ a = Ken's rating, u = the other person's rating. Use only messages both rated.
== Worked example: Ken and Lee averages
\bar{a} = \frac{1 + 5 + 2 + 4}{4} = \frac{12}{4} = 3
\bar{u} = \frac{4 + 2 + 5 + 1}{4} = \frac{12}{4} = 3
== Worked example: Ken and Lee result
w = \frac{-8}{\sqrt{10 \times 10}} = \frac{-8}{\sqrt{100}} = \frac{-8}{10} = -0.8
== Worked example: Ken and Meg result
w = \frac{4}{\sqrt{8 \times 2}} = \frac{4}{\sqrt{16}} = \frac{4}{4} = 1
== Worked example: Ken and Nan result
w = \frac{0}{\sqrt{10 \times 10}} = 0
== Worked example: overall averages
\var{Ken} = \frac{1 + 5 + 2 + 4}{4} = \frac{12}{4} = 3
\var{Lee} = \frac{4 + 2 + 5 + 1 + 2}{5} = \frac{14}{5} = 2.8
\var{Meg} = \frac{2 + 4 + 3 + 5}{4} = \frac{14}{4} = 3.5
== Worked example: prediction
P = \bar{a} + \frac{\sum (r - \bar{u}) w}{\sum |w|}
P = 3 + \frac{0.64 + 1.50}{0.8 + 1.0}
P = 3 + \frac{2.14}{1.8} = 3 + 1.19 = 4.19
== Worked example: cosine scores
\var{Lee} = \frac{4 + 10 + 0 + 10 + 4 + 0}{\sqrt{46 \times 50}} = \frac{28}{47.96} = 0.58
\var{Meg} = \frac{2 + 20}{\sqrt{46 \times 54}} = \frac{22}{49.84} = 0.44
\var{Nan} = \frac{2 + 20 + 10 + 4}{\sqrt{46 \times 46}} = \frac{36}{46} = 0.78
== Worked example: cosine prediction
P = \frac{2 \times 0.58 + 5 \times 0.44}{0.58 + 0.44} = \frac{1.16 + 2.20}{1.02} = 3.3
