== Pearson correlation between active user a and user u
w(a,u) = \frac{\sum_{i} (r(a,i) - \bar{r}_a)(r(u,i) - \bar{r}_u)}{\sqrt{\sum_{i} (r(a,i) - \bar{r}_a)^2 \times \sum_{i} (r(u,i) - \bar{r}_u)^2}}
~ The sums are over i in I(a,u), the items rated by both. w is between -1 and 1.
? It answers: do these two people's ratings go up and down together?
? First take away each person's own average, so a number says "more than usual" (+) or "less than usual" (-).
? Multiply the two numbers for each item both rated. Agreeing gives a positive result, disagreeing a negative one. Add them up.
? The bottom only rescales the total to lie between -1 (opposite tastes) and 1 (same tastes).
> Ann rates three films 5, 3, 1. Bob rates them 4, 3, 2. Both average 3.
> Take away the averages: Ann is +2, 0, -2 and Bob is +1, 0, -1.
= w = \frac{2 + 0 + 2}{\sqrt{8 \times 2}} = 1
> Bob is less extreme than Ann, but their ups and downs match exactly, so the score is 1.
== Cosine (vector) similarity
\cos(a,u) = \frac{a \cdot u}{|a| |u|}
~ Users are rating vectors, with 0 for "not rated".
? Treat each person's ratings as an arrow, and measure how closely the two arrows point the same way.
? The top is large when both people gave high ratings to the same items.
? The bottom removes the effect of how long each arrow is, so someone who rates a lot is not automatically "more similar".
> Three films, 0 means not rated. Ann is (5, 3, 0) and Bob is (4, 0, 2).
= \cos = \frac{5 \times 4 + 3 \times 0 + 0 \times 2}{\sqrt{34} \times \sqrt{20}} = 0.77
> 1 would mean the same direction. 0 would mean no rated film in common.
== Prediction 1: weighted average of ratings
P(a,i) = \frac{\sum_{u} r(u,i) w(a,u)}{\sum_{u} |w(a,u)|}
~ Assumes everyone uses the rating scale in the same way.
? You want to guess what person a would give item i. Ask other people u who have rated it.
? Do not count everyone equally. w(a,u) says how similar u is to a, so a similar person gets a bigger say.
? The top multiplies each rating by that say and adds them up.
? The bottom adds up all the say. Dividing by it brings the answer back to the rating scale (without it, more neighbours would just mean a bigger number).
? Why it is a good guess: people who agreed with you before are likely to agree with you again.
? Weakness: a negative similarity subtracts a rating and can give silly results, so use neighbours with positive similarity here.
> You are guessing Ann's rating for a film. Bob is very like Ann (0.9) and gave it 5. Cat is hardly like Ann (0.1) and gave it 2.
= P = \frac{5 \times 0.9 + 2 \times 0.1}{0.9 + 0.1} = 4.7
> A plain average would say 3.5. The weighted one says 4.7, close to Bob, because Bob's taste is the one that matches Ann's.
== Prediction 2: mean-centred weighted average (preferred)
P(a,i) = \bar{r}_a + \frac{\sum_{u} (r(u,i) - \bar{r}_u) w(a,u)}{\sum_{u} |w(a,u)|}
~ The sums are over the n neighbours who rated i.
? Some people give high marks to everything and others are harsh. A 4 does not mean the same from both.
? So for each neighbour, use how far above or below **their own** average they rated the item.
? Take the weighted average of those differences, with similar people counting more.
? Add the result to **your** average, which puts the guess on your own scale.
> Ann averages 3. Bob (similarity 0.9) averages 4 and gave the film 5, so he is +1. Cat (similarity 0.1) averages 2 and gave it 1, so Cat is -1.
= P = 3 + \frac{(+1) \times 0.9 + (-1) \times 0.1}{0.9 + 0.1} = 3.8
> Bob liked it more than usual and Bob counts most, so the guess is above Ann's usual 3.
== Significance weighting (Herlocker 1999)
w'(a,u) = \frac{\min(|I(a,u)|, 50)}{50} \times w(a,u)
? Two people can look very alike by luck when they have only a few items in common.
? So shrink the similarity when there are fewer than 50 shared items.
? With 50 or more shared items the fraction is 1 and nothing changes.
> Two people have similarity 0.9 but only 5 films in common.
= w' = \frac{5}{50} \times 0.9 = 0.09
> With 60 films in common it would stay at 0.9.
== Adjusted cosine item similarity
s(i,j) = \frac{\sum_{u} (r(u,i) - \bar{r}_u)(r(u,j) - \bar{r}_u)}{\sqrt{\sum_{u} (r(u,i) - \bar{r}_u)^2 \times \sum_{u} (r(u,j) - \bar{r}_u)^2}}
~ The sums are over users who rated both i and j.
== Item-item prediction
P(a,i) = \frac{\sum_{j} s(i,j) r(a,j)}{\sum_{j} |s(i,j)|}
~ j runs over the k items most similar to i that a has rated. With raw ratings, use only positive similarities.
== AvgIDF / MaxIDF
\var{AvgIDF}(q) = \frac{1}{|q|} \sum_{t \in q} \log \frac{N}{df(t)}
\var{MaxIDF}(q) = \max_{t} \log \frac{N}{df(t)}
~ N = documents in the collection, df(t) = documents containing t. High = specific = easier.
== Query scope
\var{Scope}(q) = \frac{|\{ d \in C : d \text{ contains a query term} \}|}{|C|}
~ "computer science": 8000/10000 = 0.80 (broad). "algorthim recusion": 30/10000 = 0.003 (misspelling or missing coverage).
== Term relatedness: average pointwise mutual information
\var{PMI}(t_1,t_2) = \log \frac{P(t_1,t_2)}{P(t_1) P(t_2)}
P(t) = \frac{df(t)}{N} \quad P(t_1,t_2) = \frac{df(t_1,t_2)}{N}
~ The predictor is the average PMI over all pairs of query terms. "neural network training" (terms co-occur) beats "neural banana training". Undefined for single-term queries.
== Collection-query similarity (SCQ, Zhao et al.; extra, not on the slides)
\var{SCQ}(t) = (1 + \ln cf(t)) \times \ln (1 + \frac{N}{df(t)})
~ SCQ(q) = the sum or max over query terms. cf = collection frequency. Higher = the query is more similar to the collection = easier.
== Clarity score
\var{Clarity}(q) = \sum_{w} P(w|Q) \log_2 \frac{P(w|Q)}{P(w|C)}
~ P(w|Q) is the word distribution of the top-k retrieved documents (weighted by retrieval score). P(w|C) is the collection distribution.
== Normalised Query Commitment (NQC)
\var{NQC}(q) = \frac{\sqrt{\frac{1}{k} \sum_{d \in \text{top-k}} (s(d) - \mu)^2}}{|s(C)|}
~ s(d) = retrieval score of d, μ = mean of the top-k scores, s(C) = score of the collection treated as one document (the normaliser).
== Statistical accuracy
\var{MAE} = \frac{1}{|T|} \sum |p(u,i) - r(u,i)|
\var{RMSE} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{|T|} \sum (p(u,i) - r(u,i))^2}
~ Over the test set T. RMSE punishes large errors more (Netflix prize metric).
== Ideal query vector (needs complete relevance knowledge)
q_{ideal} = \frac{1}{|C_R|} \sum_{d \in C_R} d - \frac{1}{N - |C_R|} \sum_{d \notin C_R} d
~ The difference of the two centroids.
== Rocchio
q_{new} = \alpha q_{orig} + \frac{\beta}{|D_R|} \sum_{d \in D_R} d - \frac{\gamma}{|D_N|} \sum_{d \in D_N} d
== Graded Rocchio
q_{new} = \alpha q_{orig} + \beta \frac{\sum_{d : f(d) > 0} f(d) d}{\sum f(d)} - \gamma \frac{\sum_{d : f(d) < 0} |f(d)| d}{\sum |f(d)|}
== Association cluster (Jaccard over documents)
M(i,j) = \frac{f(i,j)}{f(i) + f(j) - f(i,j)}
~ f(i) = number of local documents containing term i. f(i,j) = number containing both.
== Metric cluster (proximity)
M(i,j) = \sum_{(t_i, t_j)} \frac{1}{dist(t_i, t_j)}
~ Summed over pairs of occurrences. Closer occurrences contribute more.
== Scalar cluster (similar contexts)
v_i = (M(i,1), \dots, M(i,n))
S(t_i,t_j) = \frac{v_i \cdot v_j}{|v_i| |v_j|}
~ Terms that co-occur with the same other terms are related even if they never appear together (synonyms).
== RankNet pairwise loss
P(i \triangleright j) = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-(s_i - s_j))}
\var{loss} = - \log P(i \triangleright j)
~ The loss applies when i is truly more relevant. s = model score.
== Pointwise loss (regression)
L = \sum_{(q,d)} (f(x_{q,d}) - y_{q,d})^2
~ Example: label 3, prediction 2.4 gives loss 0.36.
== MMR
\var{next} = \argmax_{d \in C \setminus S} [ \lambda \cdot rel(d,q) - (1 - \lambda) \cdot \max_{d_j \in S} sim(d, d_j) ]
~ C = candidates. S = items already selected. λ is between 0 and 1 (1 = pure relevance, 0 = pure novelty).
== Precision and recall
\var{Precision} = \frac{|R \cap A|}{|A|} = \frac{TP}{TP + FP}
\var{Recall} = \frac{|R \cap A|}{|R|} = \frac{TP}{TP + FN}
== F-measure (weighted harmonic mean)
F_{\beta} = \frac{(\beta^2 + 1) P R}{\beta^2 P + R}
F_1 = \frac{2 P R}{P + R}
~ β > 1 emphasises recall, β < 1 emphasises precision.
== Basic tf-idf (Spärck Jones / Salton & Fox)
w(t,d) = f(t,d) \times \log \frac{N}{n_t}
~ f = (possibly normalised) frequency of t in d, N = number of documents, n_t = number of documents containing t.
== Max-tf normalised document weight (Salton & Buckley)
w(t,d) = \frac{tf(t,d)}{\max_{s} tf(s,d)} \times \log \frac{N}{df(t)}
== Query weight (Salton & Buckley)
w(t,q) = (0.5 + 0.5 \times \frac{tf(t,q)}{\max_{s} tf(s,q)}) \times \log \frac{N}{df(t)}
~ Every query term starts at 0.5 and rises with its frequency in the query.
== Sublinear tf
w(t,d) = 1 + \log tf(t,d) \quad \text{if tf > 0, else 0}
~ Base 10: tf 1, 10, 30, 100 gives 1, 2, 2.48, 3. 30 occurrences is not 30 times as significant as 1.
== Local x global x query
sim(q,d) = \sum_{t \in q \cap d} ntf(t,d) \times gwt(t) \times qwt(t,q)
~ ntf = normalised local frequency, gwt = global weight (idf), qwt = weight of t in the query.
== Cosine similarity
sim(q,d) = \frac{q \cdot d}{|q| |d|} = \frac{\sum_{t} w(t,q) w(t,d)}{\sqrt{\sum_{t} w(t,q)^2} \times \sqrt{\sum_{t} w(t,d)^2}}
~ Range 0 to 1 for non-negative weights.
== BM25 score of document d for query q
score(q,d) = \sum_{t \in q} \log \frac{N - df_t + 0.5}{df_t + 0.5}
\quad \times \frac{tf_{t,d} (k_1 + 1)}{tf_{t,d} + k_1 ((1 - b) + b \frac{|d|}{avgdl})} \times tf_{t,q}
~ One formula over two lines: the second line is inside the sum.
== Precision at k
P@k = \frac{\text{relevant documents in top k}}{k}
\var{R-precision} = P@R
== Average precision (AP) and MAP
\var{AP}(q) = \frac{1}{|R_q|} \sum_{k : d_k \text{ relevant}} P@k
\var{MAP} = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q} \var{AP}(q)
~ Divide by ALL relevant documents, so unretrieved relevant documents count as precision 0.
== Mean reciprocal rank
\var{MRR} = \frac{1}{|Q|} \sum_{q} \frac{1}{rank_q}
~ rank_q = rank of the first relevant document for query q.
== DCG and nDCG
\var{DCG}@k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{rel_i}{\log_2 (i + 1)}
\var{nDCG}@k = \frac{\var{DCG}@k}{\var{IDCG}@k}
~ IDCG = DCG of the ideal ordering (sorted by relevance). nDCG is between 0 and 1. A variant uses gain 2^rel - 1.
== Purity
\var{purity}(\Omega, C) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k} \max_{j} |\omega_k \cap c_j|
~ For each cluster take the count of its majority class, add them up, divide by the total number of points.
== Rand index (pair counting)
\var{RI} = \frac{TP + TN}{TP + FP + FN + TN}
~ Over all N(N-1)/2 document pairs. TP = same class and same cluster; FP = different classes but same cluster; FN = same class but different clusters; TN = different in both.
== p-norm similarity for two terms (p between 1 and infinity)
sim(d, t_1 \text{ OR } t_2) = (\frac{w_1^p + w_2^p}{2})^{\frac{1}{p}}
sim(d, t_1 \text{ AND } t_2) = 1 - (\frac{(1 - w_1)^p + (1 - w_2)^p}{2})^{\frac{1}{p}}
~ NOT t has weight (1 - w).
== PageRank
\var{PR}(W) = \frac{T}{N} + (1 - T) [ \frac{\var{PR}(W_1)}{O(W_1)} + \frac{\var{PR}(W_2)}{O(W_2)} + \dots + \frac{\var{PR}(W_n)}{O(W_n)} ]
~ W_i = pages linking to W; O(W_i) = number of outlinks of W_i; T = teleportation probability (damping factor d = 1 - T, typically 0.85); N = number of pages.
== Matrix form
a \leftarrow M^T h \quad h \leftarrow M a
a \propto M^T M a \quad h \propto M M^T h
~ M is the adjacency matrix (M_ij = 1 if page i links to page j). Repeated multiplication (power method) converges to the principal eigenvector of M^T M (authorities) and of M M^T (hubs).
== Coverage and novelty
\var{Coverage} = \frac{|AU|}{|U|}
\var{Novelty} = \frac{|New|}{|New| + |AU|}
== Zipf
f \times r \approx \text{constant} \quad f \approx \frac{C}{r}
f \propto r^{-\alpha} \quad \alpha \approx 1
~ A straight line on a log-log plot. r = rank by frequency, f = frequency.
== Heaps
M = k T^{\beta}
~ M = vocabulary size (types), T = collection size in tokens, typically 10 ≤ k ≤ 100 and 0.4 ≤ β ≤ 0.6.
